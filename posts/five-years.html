Who Learns the Hard Way Now?

Views are my own. This is personal thinking about the industry as a whole, not about Google or any team I’ve worked on.

“Explain this like I’m five.”

I’ve typed that into an AI plenty of times. So has almost everyone I know. In the same few months, I watched layoffs roll through tech and friends quit good jobs to go build something of their own.

None of that felt connected at first. Then it did. And I got curious, honestly just curious, about where it all goes. What does work look like in a few years, with AI agents everywhere? What happens to the people just starting out? And what is a university even for?

So I started asking people. When I asked a college student why she still goes to class, her first answer was “75% attendance.” I’ll come back to that.

This isn’t research. It’s me thinking out loud.

Picture a team of 25

Start with something easy to picture: a normal engineering team of 25. A couple of layers of managers, a bunch of mid-level engineers, and juniors under them. That structure exists for a reason. The work is too big for a few people, and the juniors are how you grow the next seniors.

Now add AI and fast forward a few years. What does the same team actually need?

  • One good manager who knows what really needs to be built.
  • A few mid-level engineers who already know what to ask for, now working with AI.
  • Very few juniors, mostly doing the grunt work around the AI.

That’s roughly six people doing what 25 used to. The number isn’t the interesting part. The interesting part is which role quietly goes missing. The manager stays, because deciding what to build is still hard. The mid-level engineer stays, because they can tell when the output is wrong. The junior is the one in trouble, and not because they’re bad. A junior’s whole job, economically, was to become a mid-level engineer someday. That’s the step being skipped.

What a good team looks like

I think about this a lot.

A good team has a manager who knows what actually needs to be built, and protects people from everything else. It treats teaching as part of the job, not a favour. It gives a junior two years to become good, not two weeks to become useful. And it notices when someone is struggling before it shows up in their work.

The problem is that almost none of that shows up on a spreadsheet. And the spreadsheet is what decides the team of six.

The missing step

When a senior engineer uses AI, they’re amplifying years of stuff they learned the hard way. They know what to ask. They can look at a confident, nicely formatted answer and just feel that it’s wrong, even before they can explain why.

When someone in their first year uses the same tool with a deadline on their head, it looks different. Copy, paste, it passes, move on. Nothing actually got learned.

And it’s not just at work. Back to “explain this like I’m five.” It’s brilliant for getting started. But having something explained to you like you’re five is not the same as understanding it. The struggle, the part where you sit with something you don’t get, is where the muscle actually gets built. Skip it every time and the muscle never shows up.

It’s AI plus normal human laziness plus pressure to ship faster, all at once. Together, I think people starting out now are learning less than their seniors did. And that team of six only works because those seniors learned it the old way. So where does the next batch come from?

What AI can’t do, at least not yet

Here’s the part that gives me some hope.

Picture a sales drop for a medical device in one specific region. An AI will spot it in seconds. So will a good data analyst. But spotting it isn’t the job. The job is figuring out why, and the real reason is usually human. Maybe doctors there stopped trusting it after one bad experience. Maybe a distributor quietly stopped pushing it. You only find that by talking to people and actually understanding them.

That’s empathy, and I don’t think AI takes it over anytime soon. It’s the analyst’s real job. It’s the product manager’s job. It’s what makes a senior engineer senior.

But notice who that protects: people who already have the judgement and the context. It doesn’t protect the huge number of people just starting out, in an industry moving faster than they can learn it.

What if thinking gets cheap?

If I put myself in the chair of whoever decides headcount, the maths is pretty simple. I can run 100 agents in parallel and steer each one. Agents don’t sleep. I pay a few people to steer them, and I get a huge amount of output in a short time.

For a long time, thinking was the expensive part. It was what you hired for. Now you can rent it, in parallel, overnight, as much of it as you can supervise. Value stops growing with the number of people who can think, and starts growing with the number of people who can steer. That’s a much smaller group.

Why wait on the first rung?

Maybe that’s part of why so many people I know are dropping out, or quitting, to start something. Some of it is pure excitement. But some of it, I think, is that the old ladder looks shaky. If the first rung is disappearing, why stand around waiting for it?

I don’t know yet whether that’s the smart move or just the brave one.

What happens to the middle

In India there’s a segment I think about a lot: people earning somewhere between ₹20 lakh and ₹1.5 crore a year, mostly in tech and digital work. The flashy middle class. It buys the cars, the apartments, the phones and the weekend trips. A huge part of the consumer economy leans on it.

Now run the team of six across a whole industry. If a big share of that group gets squeezed out, the damage isn’t just to them. The economy loses its customers. Meanwhile money pools where the leverage is: with whoever owns the AI platforms, and the small, efficient companies built on top of them. Neither needs many employees.

That’s why I think something like UBI eventually stops being an ideological debate and becomes plain arithmetic. Not out of kindness. Out of necessity.

So what is a university for?

Universities are still the most sought-after path. Four years, a degree, a first job. But if thinking is cheap to rent and juniors aren’t being trained anyway, I genuinely don’t know what those four years are buying.

Back to that student. Her first reason for going to class was the attendance rule. Her second was her friends. And she said agents could probably teach her faster anyway, and YouTube already does.

I think the friends and the experience matter. I’m just not sure that’s what a degree is supposed to be. If the degree stops getting you the first job, because the first job disappeared into a team of six, families are paying for the experience alone. Some can afford that. Most can’t.

The argument I can’t win

The pushback I hear most is some version of this: there will always be rich and poor. Some people see further, work harder, and take the glory, and expecting anything else is idealistic. If that’s right, this is just a reshuffle.

I beg to differ, but it’s an observation, not proof. It’s not just the tools changing. People are changing too. Short videos are eating our focus, and people want the aha moment served to them, because sitting through a one-hour class isn’t easy anymore.

That pyramid assumes the bottom keeps producing talent, the way it always has. But deep skill has always come from long, boring, focused hours, first in a classroom and then on the job. If both of those go away at the same time, the pyramid doesn’t just reshuffle who sits on top. The foundation wears away.

I think I’m right. I can’t prove it.

What I’m still asking

  • If this is where things go, what happens to people? Not jobs, people. How they spend their days, and where they get their sense of worth.
  • Where do the next senior engineers, analysts and product managers come from, if nobody pays for the years it takes to make one?
  • What happens to India’s middle class if the jobs that built it shrink?
  • Is starting something becoming the new first job?
  • And what is a university for, if not that?

If you have a better answer to any of these, I’d genuinely like to hear it.
