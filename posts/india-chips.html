India Designs the World’s Chips. So Why Doesn’t It Sell Them?

Views are my own. I work in chip design, so to be clear: everything here comes from public information, not from my job or my team.

Take the Outer Ring Road in Bangalore and count the chip company logos on the buildings. Intel. Qualcomm. Nvidia. AMD. You run out of fingers pretty quickly.

Now try to name one chip in your phone, laptop or car that an Indian company designed and sells. I tried. I couldn’t.

That gap has been bugging me for a while.

The number that doesn’t add up

Roughly a fifth of the world’s chip design engineers are based in India. Qualcomm, Intel, Nvidia, Broadcom and MediaTek all run large design centres across Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Noida. Some of the most advanced chips in the world have parts that were designed, checked and signed off here.

And yet, if you list the chip companies that actually own a product and sell it to customers, India barely shows up. We have one of the biggest chip design workforces on the planet, and almost no chip brands.

Designing a chip isn’t owning it

A design centre in Bangalore can do world-class engineering on a chip. But the decision of what to build, the specification, the patents, the customer relationship and most of the profit usually sit with the parent company somewhere else.

That’s not a complaint. It’s how the setup works. The engineering happens here. The ownership happens there. You can be excellent at the first without ever touching the second.

A chip is mostly software

This is the part people outside the industry rarely talk about. A chip on its own is useless. Before a phone maker or a car company can use it, they need the firmware, the drivers, the compiler, the development kit, and reference boards that just work.

Nvidia isn’t only GPUs. It’s CUDA, the software that made millions of developers comfortable building on its chips. A modem chip is only as good as the years of firmware running inside it.

And selling a chip to a device maker isn’t a one-time sale. It’s a promise to keep fixing bugs and shipping updates for years. Getting a design to tape-out is hard. Building and maintaining everything around it is often the bigger job, and it’s exactly the part a design centre almost never owns.

Failing fast becomes failing expensive

For a software startup, most of the tools are free or pay-as-you-go. You can be wrong cheaply and find out in weeks.

A chip startup pays before it even knows whether its idea works. Design tools and licensed building blocks, mostly from a handful of foreign companies, cost a lot, and one mistake in the silicon can mean months of delay.

How much it hurts depends a lot on which process you build on. A set of masks for a leading-edge chip can run into millions of dollars. On older, mature processes, prototyping is far cheaper, which is where many Indian startups are starting. MosChip, for example, validated key parts of its smart meter chip on a 180nm test chip. The government has also started sharing a national pool of design tools with startups, which takes some of the edge off.

Look at Netrasemi, a Thiruvananthapuram startup and one of the brightest examples we have. It was among the first startups picked for government chip design support in 2023. Its edge AI chip got working silicon in 2026, and commercial production is expected the year after. That’s roughly four years from backing to volume, and that’s a good outcome.

Software startups burn money finding users. Chip startups burn money before they get the chance to be wrong. Most startup money in India grew up on the first rhythm. I don’t think the talent was ever the missing piece. The patience was.

The safest seat in the room

There’s also a quieter reason, and it’s about people like me.

If you’re a good chip engineer in Bangalore, a global company will pay you well and give you interesting work and a stable career. Leaving that to join a chip startup means years of lower pay and a real chance the company never gets to volume. Most people, reasonably, stay.

And there’s a second effect. A modern chip is so big that it’s split into hundreds of pieces, and most engineers spend years going very deep on one of them. That’s how you build something that complex. But it also means you can spend a decade in chips without ever deciding what a chip should be, who it’s for, or how to get it into someone’s product. Those are exactly the skills a chip company needs most.

It’s starting to change

To be fair, this picture is shifting. Four Indian companies, 3rdiTech, Netrasemi, BigEndian Semiconductors and Mindgrove Technologies, taped out test chips in 2025 for surveillance cameras and had raised over ₹300 crore between them. Mindgrove has placed its first mass manufacturing order for its secure IoT chips. These are products, owned here, sold by someone here. That’s new.

The argument I can’t fully beat

The strongest pushback: maybe being the world’s design back office is enough. It pays well, it employs a lot of people, and engineering services firms here keep moving into higher-value chip work every year. Why take on the risk of owning a product?

Taiwan is the answer I keep coming back to. Not TSMC, which is a manufacturing story. MediaTek. It was spun out of UMC, another Taiwanese chipmaker, in 1997. It started with unglamorous chips for CD and DVD drives, then moved into phones. Its big break there was selling phone makers a complete package of chip plus software, so even small manufacturers could ship a phone quickly. Today it’s one of the biggest chip companies in the world.

That’s the real contrast. Taiwan’s design teams became product companies. Ours mostly became design centres for companies headquartered somewhere else. A workforce, however large, is not a moat. Salaries rise, and the work can move. A product with customers who depend on it is.

What I can’t prove is that our design centres won’t seed product companies the same way UMC seeded MediaTek. It has happened before, elsewhere.

Where I’d bet

If I had to pick where India wins first, it wouldn’t be phone processors or AI chips at the leading edge. That’s a fight against the biggest companies in the world, on the most expensive processes there are.

I’d bet on the boring chips India itself buys in huge numbers: security cameras, smart electricity meters, motor controllers for fans and EVs, broadband boxes. That’s exactly where the startups are already heading.

Three things make these a good place to start. They run on mature, cheaper processes, so one mistake doesn’t sink the company. The buyers are right here, and they increasingly care where their chips come from, especially for security cameras. And the software around them is manageable, so a small team can ship the complete package, the way MediaTek did.

Start where the chips are boring, the process is old, and the customer is next door. Then climb. That’s the MediaTek playbook, and I think it’s the most realistic one India has.

What I’m still asking

  • Who in India is actually willing to be patient: founders, investors, or the government?
  • What would it take for experienced chip engineers to leave a stable job and build something of their own?
  • And in ten years, will we still be counting other companies’ logos on the Outer Ring Road?

If you work in this space and think I’ve got it wrong, tell me.
