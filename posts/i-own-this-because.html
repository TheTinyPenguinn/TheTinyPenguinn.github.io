I Own This Because…

Not investment advice. This is about understanding what you already own, not about what anyone should buy or sell.

Here’s a small test. Open your portfolio and, for every stock in it, finish this sentence in one line: “I own this because…”

Mine had been built the way a lot of people’s are: without a strategy. A tip here, a green week there. For some of it, the most honest ending I could come up with was “it was going up that week” or “everyone around me was buying it.”

That’s not a reason. That’s a mood.

So one weekend I sat down and wrote a plan to fix it, with AI doing the heavy lifting. This is that plan.

Apps tell you what happened, not why

Every investing app shows me the same thing: a number, and whether it’s red or green. That tells me what happened. It never tells me why I own something, or whether that reason still holds. So the default becomes checking. Open the app at lunch, open it again before bed. It feels like work. It isn’t. I wanted a way to look at everything properly once in a while, and then leave it alone.

Two buckets

The whole plan starts from one split.

  • Structural Tailwind. I can say why I own it in one sentence, and the reason is bigger than the share price. An industry that’s clearly shifting, a business that’s genuinely getting better, demand that’s outrunning supply.
  • Retail Noise. Anything I can only explain with “it was moving”, “a friend mentioned it”, or “everyone was buying it”.

Noise isn’t automatically bad. But if I can’t say why I own something, I also can’t say when I should stop owning it.

The plan

Eight phases. Here’s the short version.

  • Get the data. Export stock holdings from Zerodha and mutual funds from Coin. Note what’s invested and what’s sitting idle.
  • Triage. Give both files to Claude. Ask it to put every stock in one of the two buckets with a one-line reason, and to flag mutual funds that overlap, cost too much, or lag their category.
  • Verify. For anything it wants to cut, two minutes on Screener or Tijori: return on capital, which way the promoters are moving, cash flow, debt. If the numbers agree, act on it. If they don’t, it moves to a deeper look instead.
  • Pool and wait. Add up what’s left. Don’t rush to put it back in.
  • Go deep on one. Run a full diligence on a single company, ideally in something I understand from work.
  • Attack it. Read the bear case, check the weakest point myself, and decide my exit before I enter. Then stop watching.
  • Repeat, then write. Do three or four deep dives before writing a proper memo. My note to myself here was: “Your first memos will be bad. That’s the learning.”
  • Keep refining. Add India-specific context, the latest results and news, what people are saying online, and keep tuning the prompts for as long as I hold anything.

Seven questions before I trust anything

The deep dive is a checklist I call the 7-Node Framework. Every company has to answer seven questions:

  • Promoters. Are the people running it buying or selling, and how have they spent money in the past?
  • Institutions. Are the big funds adding or leaving over the last year?
  • Tailwind. Is the market big and growing, and is there a real moat?
  • Execution. Can it actually deliver what’s in its order book or pipeline?
  • Financials. Are revenue and margins moving the right way?
  • Capital efficiency. Does it earn good returns without drowning in debt, and does it actually generate cash?
  • Valuation. Is it cheap or expensive compared to its own history and its peers?

Then a score out of ten. And the part I care about most: a bear case that attacks the weakest of the seven.

Make it argue with you

Left alone, these models are agreeable. Tell them your reason and they’ll mostly find a way to like it. The bear case is how I force the opposite. If the reason is a growing industry, what if it grows slower, or someone else takes the growth? If it’s a good business, what’s the one number that would prove it isn’t?

If a reason can’t survive a few hard questions from a chatbot, it probably wasn’t a reason.

What it gets wrong

  • It’s out of date. It doesn’t know last quarter’s results or last week’s news unless you give them to it.
  • It’s confidently wrong about smaller companies. The less that’s written about a company, the more it fills the gaps with guesses.
  • It talks about sectors, not companies. “This industry is growing” says nothing about whether one company is executing.
  • It has no sense of price. A great business can still be a bad idea at the wrong price.

That’s why the plan has a verify step in the middle and a refine step at the end. AI does the structure. I do the checking.

What’s next

I’m trying this out now, one phase at a time.

This was also meant to be one small part of WealthOS, a personal finance experiment I started for myself. Something to help me understand what I already own, not tell me what to buy.

Until then, the sentence works on its own. If you can’t finish “I own this because…”, that’s already worth knowing.
